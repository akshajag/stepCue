# StepCue guide (retrieval passages for "Ask StepCue")

Each section is one passage. Keep them practical, about the device, and free of medical advice.

## What a freeze episode is
StepCue logs a freeze episode when the ankle sensor sees the feet stop moving forward mid-walk, often with a trembling motion, while the person is trying to walk. The episode ends when steady stepping resumes. Very short readings (under one second) are often sensor noise rather than a real freeze.

## How the walking cue works
When a freeze is detected StepCue starts a steady rhythm: the ankle light flashes on each beat, and if wrist vibration is on, the wrist buzzes on each beat too. The wearer steps in time with the beat. The cue stops by itself once walking is steady again.

## Cue tempo (bpm)
The cue tempo is the number of beats per minute. StepCue starts near the wearer's normal walking pace and the care agent adjusts it a little at a time, by at most 20 bpm per change, toward the tempo after which walking resumed fastest. The allowed range is 60 to 130 bpm.

## Wrist vibration
Wrist vibration adds a felt beat to the ankle light. The care agent turns it on when the light alone has not been enough, for example after a freeze longer than 4 seconds or slow recoveries, and turns it back off after several quick recoveries in a row.

## Recovery time
Recovery time is how long it took from the start of the cue until steady walking resumed. Under about 2 seconds means the current cue is working well. Over about 3 seconds, or getting longer through the day, is a pattern the care agent responds to.

## Alert levels
Normal means nothing needs doing. Keep an eye out means a pattern is worth watching, such as several freezes close together or slower recoveries. Check in means a freeze is happening right now and has lasted a long time, or freezes are frequent and long, so a caregiver should check on the wearer. Alerts calm back to normal by themselves after a quiet period.

## Freezes during turns
StepCue notes when a freeze began during a turn or just after one. If most freezes begin in turns, that is a pattern worth sharing with the care team. It is a pattern in the data, not a cause.

## Phone check-ins
The wearer's phone page has an I'm OK button, a two-tap I need help button, and a question about how walking feels today (easy, so-so, hard). A help request sends the caregiver a push notification and, if set up, a phone call. Messages never include names or location.

## Confirming a freeze on the phone
After a freeze, the wearer's phone may ask "Did you just freeze?". A yes confirms the episode. A no marks it as a possible false alarm, which helps the care agent avoid over-reacting to sensor noise.

## Wearing the ankle sensor
The ankle sensor should sit snugly on the outside of the ankle with the strap tight enough not to slide. A loose strap can cause flickering readings or very short false freezes. If the dashboard says the signal looks uncertain, check the strap first.

## When the dashboard says no signal
If no data has arrived for a while the dashboard shows no signal instead of the last reading. Check that the waist hub is on, the laptop is connected to the StepCue Wi-Fi, and the ankle sensor battery is charged.

## Privacy
StepCue keeps location on the laptop. Requests to NVIDIA's models have names, emails, phone numbers, coordinates and addresses removed first. Alerts never include names or location.

## What StepCue does not do
StepCue is an assistive cueing and awareness tool. It does not diagnose, does not give medication or treatment advice, and does not replace the wearer's doctor or care team. Questions about diagnosis, medication or treatment should go to the wearer's doctor.

## Practical tips for caregivers
Clear walkways and good lighting reduce the places where a freeze can turn into a fall. Doorways and tight turns are common freeze spots; leaving extra room to turn helps. If the wearer prefers, counting out loud in time with the cue can reinforce the rhythm.
